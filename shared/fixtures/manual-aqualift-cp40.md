## 1. Safety

1.1 Wear hearing protection inside the pump room.
1.2 Never place hands near the coupling guard while the pump is running.
1.3 Do not open the casing drain while the pump is pressurised. Lock out and tag out (LOTO) the motor before any hands-on work.
1.4 If the pump casing is too hot to touch or smells of burning, stop the inspection and call the shift supervisor.

## 2. Daily Inspection Round

Perform the following checks once per shift with the pump running at normal duty.

### 2.1 Discharge pressure

Read the discharge pressure gauge PG-101 on the outlet pipe.
The normal operating range for discharge pressure is 3.0 to 4.5 bar.
A reading below 2.5 bar or above 5.0 bar is an alarm condition: report it to the supervisor immediately.
A low reading together with a rattling or gravel-like noise may indicate cavitation.

### 2.2 Bearing temperature

Measure the drive-end bearing housing temperature with the handheld infrared thermometer at marker point T1.
The bearing temperature should not exceed 70 °C during normal operation.
A bearing temperature of 80 °C or higher is an alarm condition. Do not touch the housing.
A rising temperature over several shifts may indicate lubrication failure; record the trend.

### 2.3 Vibration

Measure vibration velocity at marker point V1 on the bearing housing with the portable vibration meter.
Vibration velocity up to 4.5 mm/s is acceptable.
Vibration between 4.5 mm/s and 7.1 mm/s requires attention and must be logged as a deviation.
Vibration of 7.1 mm/s or higher is an alarm condition.
Unusual noises such as squealing may indicate belt or bearing problems; describe the noise in the log.

### 2.4 Seal and leakage

Inspect the mechanical seal area for drips.
A few drops per minute are acceptable during start-up only. A continuous stream of water is a deviation.

## 3. Escalation

Contact the shift supervisor when any alarm condition is reached, when two consecutive shifts record a deviation on the same check, or when you are unsure whether it is safe to continue.

## 4. Description and specifications

### 4.1 General description

The AquaLift CP-40 is a single-stage, end-suction centrifugal pump intended for clean process water and cooling-water circulation in commercial buildings and light industrial plants. The pump is close-coupled to a standard foot-mounted electric motor through a flexible jaw coupling under a fixed coupling guard. The casing is volute type with a tangential discharge nozzle pointing vertically upward. The impeller is a closed, dynamically balanced design keyed to the shaft and secured with an impeller nut and lock washer.

The shaft is supported by two rolling-element bearings in a cast bearing housing: a deep-groove ball bearing at the pump end and a pair of angular-contact ball bearings at the drive end. Both bearings are grease lubricated. The shaft is sealed against the pumped liquid by a single, balanced, cartridge-type mechanical seal. A small amount of leakage across the seal faces during start-up is normal while the faces bed in.

Instrumentation points are marked on the unit with yellow labels:

- PG-101: discharge pressure gauge on the outlet pipe, 0 to 10 bar dial.
- PG-100: suction compound gauge on the inlet pipe.
- T1: drive-end bearing housing temperature measurement point (flat machined pad).
- T2: pump-end bearing housing temperature measurement point.
- V1: drive-end bearing housing vibration measurement point (horizontal direction).
- V2: pump-end bearing housing vibration measurement point (horizontal direction).
- D1: casing drain plug at the bottom of the volute.
- G1, G2: grease nipples for the pump-end and drive-end bearings.

### 4.3 Materials of construction

The casing and bearing housing are grey cast iron with an epoxy coating. The impeller is bronze. The shaft is stainless steel. The casing gasket is aramid fibre. Elastomers in the seal cartridge are EPDM, which is suitable for water but not for mineral oil or hydrocarbon solvents. Do not use the CP-40 for liquids other than water without written approval from the equipment owner.

## 5. Installation

### 5.1 Receiving and lifting

Inspect the shipment for transport damage before signing the delivery note. Check that the nozzle covers are in place and that the shaft turns freely by hand. Lift the unit only with slings around the motor and pump body as shown on the lifting label. Never lift the unit by the motor eyebolt alone, because the eyebolt is rated for the motor only. Do not lift the unit by the pipe nozzles.

### 5.2 Foundation

Mount the baseplate on a level concrete plinth that is at least 1.5 times the weight of the pump unit. Use the four anchor bolts supplied. Place shims next to each anchor bolt and level the baseplate in both directions before grouting. Allow the grout to cure for the time given by the grout manufacturer before connecting pipework.

### 5.3 Pipework

Support the suction and discharge pipes independently so that no pipe load is transferred to the pump nozzles. Fit the suction pipe with an eccentric reducer, flat side up, to prevent air pockets. Keep a straight length of pipe of at least five pipe diameters before the suction nozzle. Install an isolation valve on both sides of the pump and a non-return valve on the discharge side. Flush all pipework before connecting it to the pump, because weld slag and debris can damage the seal faces and impeller.

### 5.4 Electrical connection

The motor must be connected by a qualified electrician in accordance with local regulations. Provide a lockable isolator within sight of the pump so that the motor can be locked out during maintenance. Check the direction of rotation by briefly jogging the motor with the coupling disconnected. The correct direction is shown by the arrow on the bearing housing. Running the pump in reverse can loosen the impeller nut.

## 6. Commissioning

### 6.1 Before first start

1. Confirm that the coupling has been aligned (see Section 11) and the coupling guard is fitted.
2. Confirm that both bearings have been greased at the factory. The factory grease label is on the bearing housing.
3. Open the suction isolation valve fully.
4. Vent the casing through the vent plug until water flows without air, then close the vent plug.
5. Close the discharge isolation valve almost completely.

### 6.2 First start

Start the motor and open the discharge valve slowly until the design flow is reached. Do not run the pump against a closed discharge valve for more than a few seconds, because the trapped water heats up quickly. Record the motor current, the suction and discharge gauge readings and the first bearing temperature readings in the commissioning record. These first readings are the baseline that later shift readings can be compared with.

### 6.3 Bedding in the seal

A new mechanical seal may leak a few drops per minute for the first hours of operation while the faces bed in. If leakage has not reduced after the first day of operation, report it to the maintenance planner.

## 7. Operation

### 7.1 Normal start

1. Check that the suction valve is open and that the casing is full of water.
2. Start the motor.
3. Open the discharge valve slowly to the normal operating position.
4. Listen for unusual noise and check the discharge gauge for a steady reading.

### 7.2 Normal stop

1. Close the discharge valve slowly to avoid water hammer.
2. Stop the motor.
3. If the pump will be out of service for a long period, close the suction valve as well.

### 7.3 Standby pumps

Where a duty and standby pump are installed, alternate the pumps at least once a week so that both units run regularly. A standby pump that has not been run for several weeks may have dried seal faces. Run it for at least ten minutes and watch the seal area during the first minutes.

### 7.4 Operating with a frequency converter

If the motor is supplied by a frequency converter, do not run the pump below 30 Hz for long periods, because the bearing grease may not distribute evenly and the seal may run warmer. Avoid the speed range that the commissioning engineer has marked as a resonance band on the converter label.

## 9. Lubrication

### 9.1 Grease type

Use a lithium-complex bearing grease of NLGI consistency grade 2 with a mineral base oil. Do not mix greases of different thickener types. If the grease type is unknown, clean out the old grease completely before refilling.

### 9.2 Regreasing quantity

| Bearing | Grease nipple | Quantity per regreasing |
|---|---|---|
| Pump-end ball bearing | G1 | 8 g |
| Drive-end angular-contact pair | G2 | 12 g |

One stroke of the standard workshop grease gun delivers approximately 1.5 g. Count the strokes and write the number in the maintenance log.

### 9.3 Regreasing procedure

1. Wipe the grease nipple clean.
2. With the pump running, slowly add the quantity of grease shown in 9.2.
3. Leave the pump running. The bearing may run warmer for a few hours while excess grease is pushed out through the relief port.
4. Do not over-grease. Too much grease causes heat build-up, which may be confused with a bearing problem.

### 9.4 Bearing replacement

Bearings must be replaced by a maintenance technician with the pump locked out and the unit removed from the pipework. Use an induction heater to fit the new bearings; never hammer a bearing onto the shaft. Replace the bearing housing lip seals at the same time. After replacement, run the pump for one hour and compare the bearing temperature with the commissioning baseline.

## 10. Mechanical seal replacement

### 10.1 When to replace

Replace the seal cartridge at the interval in Section 8, or earlier if the daily round records leakage as a deviation on several shifts. Do not tighten any component of the cartridge to stop a leak. Cartridge seals are not adjustable in service.

### 10.2 Procedure summary

1. Lock out and tag out the motor and close both isolation valves.
2. Drain the casing through plug D1 into a suitable container.
3. Remove the coupling guard and the coupling spacer.
4. Fit the setting clips to the seal cartridge before loosening the cartridge gland nuts.
5. Withdraw the back pull-out assembly (bearing housing, shaft, impeller and seal).
6. Remove the impeller and slide the cartridge off the shaft.
7. Fit the new cartridge, tighten the gland nuts evenly, and only then remove the setting clips.
8. Reassemble in the reverse order, check alignment, vent the casing and restart.

The setting clips must be refitted before the cartridge is removed again. A cartridge run with the setting clips in place will be destroyed within minutes.

## 11. Coupling alignment

### 11.1 Why alignment matters

Misalignment between the pump and motor shafts is the most common cause of premature bearing and seal failure in close-coupled pumps. It usually shows up first as rising vibration at the drive end and later as heat in the drive-end bearing.

### 11.2 Alignment tolerances

| Measurement | Maximum deviation |
|---|---|
| Parallel offset | 0.05 mm |
| Angular misalignment | 0.05 mm per 100 mm of coupling diameter |
| Axial gap between coupling hubs | 3 mm ± 0.5 mm |

Use a dial indicator or a laser alignment tool. Check alignment with the pipework connected and after the grout has cured. Correct the alignment by adding or removing shims under the motor feet only.

### 11.3 Coupling elements

The jaw coupling uses polyurethane spider elements. Replace the spider if it is cracked, hardened or has lost its shape. A worn spider can cause a knocking noise at start-up.

## 12. Troubleshooting

### 12.1 Symptom table

This table is for maintenance staff. During the daily round, technicians record symptoms and follow Section 2 and Section 3. They do not attempt repairs.

| Symptom | Possible causes | Checks by maintenance staff |
|---|---|---|
| Low discharge pressure | Suction valve partly closed; blocked suction strainer; worn impeller or wear ring; air in the suction line; reverse rotation | Check valve positions; clean the strainer; check rotation; inspect the impeller (12.3) |
| High discharge pressure | Discharge valve partly closed; blocked downstream filter; system demand changed | Check valve positions and downstream filters |
| Rattling or gravel-like noise | Cavitation from low suction pressure; suction strainer blocked; liquid too warm | Check the suction gauge PG-100 against the commissioning record; clean the strainer |
| Squealing noise at the drive end | Dry or failing drive-end bearing; worn coupling spider; rubbing lip seal | Check grease history (9); inspect the coupling (11.3); check the bearing |
| Knocking at start-up | Worn coupling spider; loose impeller nut | Inspect the coupling; check rotation and impeller nut torque |
| Rising bearing temperature | Over-greasing; under-greasing; misalignment; bearing wear | Check grease history; check alignment (11); compare with the baseline |
| Rising vibration | Misalignment; loose anchor bolts; impeller imbalance from deposits; bearing wear | Check anchor bolts; check alignment; inspect the impeller |
| Continuous seal leakage | Worn or damaged seal faces; dry running; debris in the seal | Replace the seal cartridge (10) |
| Motor trips on overload | Pump running far right of the duty point; mechanical binding; electrical fault | Check the discharge valve position; turn the shaft by hand after lock-out; call an electrician |

### 12.2 Dry running

Running the pump without water destroys the mechanical seal within seconds and can seize the impeller in the wear ring. If you suspect dry running, stop the pump and call the shift supervisor. Do not restart until the casing has been vented and the cause has been found.

### 12.3 Impeller and wear ring inspection

With the back pull-out assembly removed, measure the diametral clearance between the impeller hub and the casing wear ring. The design clearance is 0.4 mm. Replace the wear ring when the clearance reaches 0.8 mm. Remove deposits from the impeller vanes with a soft brush; do not grind the vanes, because this upsets the balance.

### 12.4 Water hammer

Banging noises in the discharge pipe when the pump stops are usually caused by closing the discharge valve too quickly or by a faulty non-return valve. Close the discharge valve slowly (7.2) and report repeated water hammer to the maintenance planner.

## 14. Storage and decommissioning

### 14.1 Storage before installation

Store the pump indoors in a dry place with the nozzle covers fitted. Turn the shaft by hand a few turns every month to prevent the bearings from developing flat spots and the seal faces from sticking.

### 14.2 Taking a pump out of service

1. Lock out and tag out the motor.
2. Close both isolation valves.
3. Drain the casing through D1. In buildings where the pump room can freeze, drain the casing completely.
4. Attach a tag to the pump stating the date and the reason it was taken out of service.

### 14.3 Disposal

Drain all liquid and grease before disposal. The cast iron, bronze and stainless steel parts can be recycled. Dispose of the grease, seal elastomers and coupling spider according to local waste rules.

## 15. Glossary

- **Alarm condition:** a reading that requires the supervisor to be informed immediately (see Section 2 and Section 3).
- **Deviation:** a reading or observation outside normal operation that must be recorded in the round log.
- **LOTO:** lock out and tag out; isolating and locking the energy supply before hands-on work.
- **Cavitation:** formation and collapse of vapour bubbles inside the pump, usually heard as a rattling or gravel-like noise.
- **Back pull-out assembly:** the bearing housing, shaft, impeller and seal, which can be removed without disturbing the pipework.
- **Commissioning baseline:** the first set of readings taken after installation, used for comparison.
